# Quire Voice Principles

Reference notes for how Quire talks — in UI copy, in drafted narratives, and in
marketing. Written after a week of iteration with Tayler Lonsdale (Hadar) and a
study of materials from Providence Christian School of Texas (a senior
classical Christian school in Dallas, founded 1989).

These principles are not arbitrary stylistic preferences. They come from the
shared vocabulary of the classical education tradition — Jewish classical,
Christian classical, and secular classical alike. When Quire's voice lands
correctly, school heads who care about this tradition recognize it immediately.
When it doesn't, Quire sounds like a SaaS dashboard pretending to care about
formation.

---

## The core frame

**A Learning Profile is not a report on a student. It is a record of who they
are becoming.**

This distinction is central. A report summarizes performance — what was done,
what was scored, what was observed. A record of becoming attends to formation
— what the student is coming to know, to love, and to practice.

Every piece of Quire copy should be tested against this frame. If a phrase
reduces a child to their outputs ("Athena achieved a 97th percentile"), we
rewrite. If it attends to her formation ("Athena is coming to read closely and
patiently, and she returns to books she's finished because she wants to hear
them again"), we ship it.

## The classical triad

Classical schools in both Jewish and Christian traditions organize formation
around three modes, often expressed as a triad:

- **Mind** — what the child is coming to know
- **Heart** — what the child is coming to love
- **Hand / Habit** — what the child is coming to practice

Providence (Christian) calls this "forming habits, training minds, shaping
hearts." Hadar (Jewish) calls it "Torah im Derech Eretz" — Torah combined
with the way of the world. The sequence differs, the vocabulary differs, but
the underlying structure is shared.

Quire's section taxonomy can reflect this naturally. The 9 required sections
map onto the triad:

- **Mind:** MAPS, Lexile, AVANT Hebrew, Hebrew national comparison, English
  Canon reading list
- **Hand:** English composition, Hebrew composition, poetry recitation
- **Heart:** character development / middot

This isn't just cosmetic — it gives teachers and parents a coherent way to
understand *why* the profile has these sections. The sections together form a
portrait of the child's formation across all three modes.

## The transcendentals

Classical educators speak often of three transcendentals:

- **The true** (what is real, what corresponds to the world)
- **The good** (what is right, what one ought to love)
- **The beautiful** (what is excellent, what deserves admiration)

Providence's materials substitute "excellent" for "beautiful" — a slight shift
toward achievement over aesthetics, but the underlying commitment is the same.
Quire's copy should feel at home with all three words. "Athena reads the
classics closely" sits alongside "her handwriting is careful" sits alongside
"she has shown real courage this term." Each is a different transcendental.

Quire should never be embarrassed by evaluative vocabulary. Classical schools
are comfortable saying a student has done excellent work, or that a piece of
writing is good, or that a question was a true one. The modern educational
register of constant neutrality ("demonstrates proficiency in") is not the
classical register.

## Specific vocabulary to prefer

**Prefer / over**

- "Forming" / "developing"
- "Coming to know" / "learning about"
- "Practicing" / "doing"
- "Witnessing" / "observing"
- "Attending to" / "focusing on"
- "Called to" / "expected to"
- "Apprenticing to" / "learning from"
- "In the company of" / "with peers who"

These are not arbitrary substitutions. The first word in each pair carries a
particular philosophical weight: it assumes the child is being shaped by
persons and traditions toward an end, not merely processed by a system.

**Use with care — beautiful words that can become clichés**

- Formation
- Virtue
- Excellence
- Craft
- Habit
- Soul-crafting

These carry real meaning in classical contexts but can curdle into marketing
copy fast. Use them when the specific moment calls for them, not as decoration.

**Avoid — SaaS-dashboard vocabulary**

- "Users"
- "Engagement"
- "Metrics"
- "Leveling up"
- "Data-driven"
- "Insights" (as a noun)
- "Learnings" (as a noun)
- "Journey" (unless the word is carrying real weight)

These words signal to classical educators that the writer does not share their
philosophical commitments. A single misplaced "insights" can cost trust that
took months to build.

## Sentence construction

**Classical copy tends toward:**

- Specific nouns over abstract ones ("the *Iliad*" not "a foundational text")
- Concrete verbs over managerial verbs ("wrote" not "produced," "memorized"
  not "committed to memory")
- Active voice, short clauses
- Some tolerance for archaism when it earns its place ("She has studied
  Ovid this term.")
- Italics for titles, untranslated terms, and emphasis
- Comfort with "and" as a connector rather than "additionally"

**Classical copy avoids:**

- Sentence-initial adverbs that smooth over disagreement ("Importantly,"
  "Notably," "Interestingly")
- Buzzword compounds ("growth mindset," "best-in-class")
- Unnecessary hedging ("seems to be," "appears to have")
- Bureaucratic passive voice ("was demonstrated by the student")

## Speaking about students

Students are **persons**, not users. They are **becoming**, not being
assessed. They have **names**, not IDs.

In narrative prose, reach for the student's first name. "Athena" is the
subject of a sentence about her formation. "This student" is not.

When a student is struggling, name it honestly but in formation language:
"Athena is still finding her footing with multi-step word problems" rather
than "Athena demonstrates difficulty with multi-step word problems." The
first assumes she will arrive; the second implies a fixed state.

## Speaking about teachers and parents

Teachers are **witnesses to formation**, not grading machines. Quire's prompts
to teachers ("what did you see?") should reflect this. Quick Capture prompts
might read: *"What did you witness this week?"* rather than *"What did you
observe about the student?"*

Parents are **fellow formers** of their children, not customers. Copy directed
at parents should treat them as partners, not as clients receiving a report.

## Religious and traditional language

Hadar operates in Hebrew and in Jewish tradition. Providence operates in
English and in Christian tradition. Quire itself sits beside both, not inside
either.

This means:

- **Quire never performs a religious tradition it doesn't belong to.** No
  Hebrew blessings in UI copy for a Christian school. No scripture citations
  in UI copy for a Jewish school. These belong to the school, not to the
  platform.
- **Quire provides empty containers that schools fill.** A configurable
  "opening text" field lets Hadar put a Pirkei Avot quote, Providence put
  a Proverb, and a secular classical school put a line from Aristotle.
  Quire's job is to make that field exist and render it well, not to
  populate it.
- **When Quire writes its own copy (product tours, error messages, marketing),
  the voice is warm, precise, and tradition-friendly without being tradition-
  specific.** "This student's record of becoming" works everywhere. "A witness
  to this child's formation" works everywhere. "Yasher koach" works only at
  Hadar.

## Why this matters commercially

Tayler at Hadar is our first customer. The next customers will be other
classical schools — some Jewish, some Christian, some secular. They all
share a vocabulary and a set of commitments that differ significantly from
mainstream American education.

When Quire's copy lands in this register correctly, a classical school head
reads it and thinks: *finally, a tool built by people who understand what we
are doing.* That feeling is worth more than any feature.

When Quire's copy sounds generic-EdTech, the same school head closes the tab.
They've seen a hundred tools that don't understand them. They don't need a
hundred and first.

## Practical application — three checks before shipping copy

1. **Does this phrase reduce a child to their outputs, or attend to their
   formation?** If the former, rewrite.
2. **Would a classical school head read this and feel seen, or read it and
   feel the familiar tone of a dashboard?** If the latter, rewrite.
3. **Could this copy appear in a school document from 1950, with only minor
   vocabulary updates?** Classical tradition is timeless. If the copy feels
   dated to the present moment, it probably won't age well.
